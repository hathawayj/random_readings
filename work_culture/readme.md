## Collaboration, Ownership, and Fixing Things

I’ve been thinking a lot about how we work together, especially in settings that claim to be “collaborative.” The pieces linked here argue that most modern organizations have turned collaboration into an ideology: we optimize for meetings, alignment, and consensus, but the high‑quality work usually comes from individuals or very small groups with real ownership and accountability.

I don’t fully buy the motto that we should “collaborate less.” To me, the problem isn’t collaboration itself; it’s unthinking, default collaboration that dilutes responsibility and slows everything down. What we actually need is intentional collaboration in service of a clearly owned project that can move far and fast.

The first article connects to the concept of culture in our workplaces. This Mark Atwood essay describes “error‑correctors” as the people who are willing to notice when something is wrong, say so, and push for a fix, even when institutions would rather preserve short‑term stability. In my experience, those people are invaluable. Some of us are wired to walk into a room, a codebase, a process, or a model and immediately feel what’s off. The trick is to pair that hunger to fix things with humility about our own domain limits, and with a discipline of seeing the problem as the problem, not the person.

If we want better teams and better institutions, we have to design for both explicit ownership and empowered error‑correctors. That means giving individuals real responsibility, celebrating people who find and fix issues, and creating space for mindful attention rather than mindless adherence to current constraints or politics. 

__Can we design our teams and rituals so that ownership is explicit, error‑correctors are empowered, and collaboration is a mindful tool—not a mindless ideology?__

#### My favorite quotes

> “Communication matters, and shared context matters. But there’s a huge difference between communication and collaboration as infrastructure to support individual, high-agency ownership, and communication and collaboration as the primary activity of an organization. Which, if we’re honest, is what most collaboration-first cultures have actually built. They’ve constructed extraordinarily sophisticated machinery for the social management of work, without actually doing the work they’re socializing about.“

> The collaboration industry has spent a fortune obscuring a dirty truth: most complex, high-quality work is done by individuals or very small groups operating with clear authority and sharp accountability, then rationalized into the language of teamwork afterward.

> The Apollo Guidance Computer came from a team at MIT small enough to have real ownership, hierarchical enough that Margaret Hamilton's name could go on the error-detection routines she personally designed.

> Collaboration-as-ideology has made ownership and responsibility feel antisocial, which is a hell of a thing, given that ownership is the only mechanism that gets anything across the finish line.

__I am not sure I believe the following guidance/motto.__

> The point is, if you aren’t actively attempting to collaborate less, you are probably collaborating too much by default and hurting your ability to go far, fast.

__I would say,__ "The point is, if you aren’t actively attempting to collaborate INTENTIONALLY, you are probably NOT collaborating by default and hurting your PROJECT’S ability to go far, fast."

## Because it is Wrong

- [Because it is wrong article by Mark Atwood](https://markatwood.substack.com/p/because-its-wrong?r=1fx3hb&utm_medium=ios&triedRedirect=true)

> The exclusion of error-correctors is an institutional choice to trade long-term accuracy for short-term stability. It is a rational choice in any individual instance (the political cost of the correction exceeds the cost of the error in this specific case). It is a catastrophic choice in aggregate (the accumulated errors eventually exceed the institution’s capacity to absorb surprise).

> The error-corrector lives in that gap. It’s not a comfortable place. But someone has to live there, because the errors don’t fix themselves, and the institutions that let the errors accumulate eventually discover that reality doesn’t care about stability.

> Reality cares about accuracy.

> The error-corrector has been trying to tell you this.

> The question was never “why is this so important to you.”

> The question was always “is it wrong.”

I love a culture built on fixing things. This idea has been one of the most important aspects of my career. I realized many years ago that I could choose to do many things, but that there were certain things I was never going to build the skills needed to excel. Meaning, the skill of seeing the thing that needed to be fixed in that domain. My brother can walk into any room or look at any car and instantly see or feel that the floors aren't level and the paint isn't original. I would trust him endlessly in this space and relish when he notes a discrepancy.  I think we need teams built around people who see problems and take joy in solving them. I wonder how we could celebrate fixes even more. 

The trick is for each of us not to get too overconfident in our ability to see the fix in one space, thinking we can fix any space. I have succumbed to that concept at times. We need to be hungry enough to pay attention, take ownership, and speak up so we can fix problems. We are humble enough to see the problem as the issue, not the person or the politics.  One area that gets me at times is my impatience to listen to others when they can feel an issue but cannot quite explain it. The key to this whole article seems to rest on creating a space that is mindful and open to novelty. 

> Mindlessness refers to the unquestioning acceptance of any one set of constraints or axioms that algorithmically ‘determine’ the problem-solving steps one needs to take in order to produce the desired behavior. Mindfulness and mindlessness cannot, therefore, be traded off linearly against each other. The moment we have accepted - unconditionally - a set of constraints or objectives, we have ceased to become mindful and have instead become mindless.
